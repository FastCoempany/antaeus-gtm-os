02

Why the Behance grid is the wrong model here

Behance indexes millions of projects by hundreds of thousands of authors. Uniform 16:9 tiles, an author avatar, a like count and a view count are correct there — uniformity is what makes a feed scannable, and the metrics are real because they are comparable across the whole platform.

Move that interface onto a page showing three brands you own yourself and every one of those properties inverts.

  • Uniformity destroys the asset. The entire argument these three make together is that they look nothing like each other. A monochrome eyewear label, a blue-and-black streetwear brand, and a four-fluorescent pop-art line is a range claim. Flatten them into identical tiles under shared chrome and you have deleted the evidence.
  • A grid's job is rejection, and there is nothing to reject. Dense uniform grids exist so the eye can discard fast. With three items nobody is discarding. The grid stops being a filter and becomes a stage with two-thirds of it empty.
  • Borrowed metrics read as vanity or as small numbers. Likes and views work where they are comparable. On your own site they are either unverifiable or — worse — honest and low.

The move

Keep the grid. Change what a cell contains. A cell stops being a card about a brand and becomes a portal rendered in that brand — its own ground, its own type, its own palette — held inside a shell that is deliberately, totally neutral.

Call it the gallery wall. Nobody paints the wall to match the painting, and nobody hangs three paintings in matching frames to make them look related.

Visual & UI — the wall and the work

The shell is a fixed, quiet system: one ground, one chrome typeface, one rule weight, one label plate, one gutter. Everything loud happens inside a cell.

Decided — the wall is light

My first draft argued for near-black on those numbers. You called it the other way, and having gone back through the assets I think you are right and my reading was incomplete. The thing I had not checked: the entire Darkest Shades product library — all 318 frames — is shot on a white lightbox sweep. On a dark wall every one of those would have been a glaring white rectangle punched into black. On a light wall they sit into the page as photographs.

So the wall is #EFEEEC, a warm near-neutral I would call gallery plaster. Not pure white, which is harsh under the fluorescents and makes the grey sweeps look dingy; not Digs' cool #F0F5FF, which would make the wall look like it belongs to one of the three. Ink sits on it at 15.93:1.

4.1b — What a light wall costs, and the one fix that pays for it

The light wall creates exactly one problem, and it is the same problem twice. Measured against #EFEEEC:

The fix, and why it is better than what the dark wall gave me

Every cell gets a mount — a 1px ink hairline at 15.93:1 against the wall. Light-on-light has no boundary, so you draw one.

This is not a compromise, it is the museum-correct answer: a gallery mounts work, and the mount is what separates the work from the wall. On a dark wall each cell would have been defined by luminance alone and there would have been no mount at all. Here the mount becomes the single structural device that makes three clashing brands read as one page — unifying by structure rather than by style, which is the whole thesis of this document. Your call produced the better system.

  • Brand colour fills shapes; it never sets type on the wall. On a light ground all four Puff Junction colours fail as text (1.17–2.01:1) but all four are excellent as fills under a black label (10.45–18.01:1). The accent takeover therefore changes the fill of the index plate and the mount, never the colour of running text.
  • Digs blue is the one exception and can be type at 7.21:1. No lightened twin is needed on a light wall — #6076FF from the previous draft is retired.
  • Labels on fills: black on all four Puff Junction colours and on the DS sweep; white on Digs blue. Fixed, never per-designer judgement.

4.2 — An asymmetric grid, because three cells cannot fill a symmetrical one

Three equal columns reads like a template with a row missing. Instead: a 12-column editorial grid where each brand claims the span its material actually needs. The composition becomes an act of art direction — which is precisely the thing Behance cannot do, because it does not know what is inside any tile. You do.

Desktop ≥ 1024px on the #EFEEEC wall. Each cell renders in its own brand system and carries the 1px ink mount; the gutters and the wall do not change.

  • Darkest Shades takes 7 columns and the most height. It is the most photographic of the three and its imagery is editorial fashion and lightbox product — it needs scale and air or it reads as stock.
  • Digs takes 5. It is graphic and typographic rather than photographic. It holds up dense, and the blue does more work in a smaller field than a larger one.
  • Puff Junction takes the full 12 as a band. A maximalist pop-art system in a small box reads as cramped and shouty. Given width it reads as confident. This is also the only cell that can carry all four fluorescents at once without the page fighting.

4.3 — One brand live at a time

The signature interaction, and the thing that makes this an instrument rather than a list. On hover or keyboard focus — and on scroll-into-view at touch widths — the neutral chrome adopts that brand's accent — as a fill. The index plate, the mount and the open-case affordance flood with Slime, or #0042E5, or ink black, and the label on that fill flips to whichever of black or white clears 4.5:1 (§4.1b). Running text never changes colour.

The discipline that stops it becoming a carnival: exactly one accent is live at any moment, and the wall itself never changes. Range gets dramatised without three brands shouting over each other.

  • Never unify the imagery. No shared grade, no duotone, no shared filter. That is the flattening mistake in a different costume.
  • Do unify four things: the available aspect ratios (cap at three), the corner treatment, the label-plate format, and the gutter. That is enough to read as one page.
  • Chrome type is none of the six. Setting the shell in Owners Wide implies the wall belongs to Darkest Shades. Pick a seventh, neutral face for chrome only, and let the brand faces live strictly inside their own cells and case pages.

4.5 — The Darkest Shades gallery, located

You asked for the real photographs rather than renders. The archive holds 318 lightbox frames of the manufactured product. I scored every one for a clean sweep, a centred subject and sharpness, then picked six that span the colourways and the angles. These are the actual files, not stand-ins.

The second frame is the most valuable one on this page for your audience: it shows DARKEST SHADES stamped into the temple. That single detail is the difference between a design project and a product.

What this settles about the wall

Every one of the 318 frames sits on a white-to-grey sweep measuring around #C6C6C6. That is a 1.47 edge against the wall — which is why the mount in §4.1b is not optional, and why a dark wall would have turned this entire library into bright rectangles.

4.6 — Motion

One orchestrated moment, not scattered effects. The accent takeover is the whole motion budget: a 180–220ms ease on colour and a 1–2% scale on the cell's media. No parallax, no scroll-jacking, no staggered letter reveals. Everything honours prefers-reduced-motion, where the takeover becomes an instant state change rather than a transition — the information still arrives, the movement does not.

This is where most portfolios quietly fail. Behance gives you title, author, likes, views — a marketplace's semantics. A maker needs different ones. Every cell carries the same four-part plate, and the fourth part is the entire content strategy.

5.1 — The evidence line, written three times

The rule: a number, or a thing that had to physically happen. Never an adjective, never a process noun. Write it as though the reader will only read one line on the page — because most of them will.

  • Darkest Shades — “323 lightbox shots of a product that exists. Thirty-one 3D files and twenty technical drawings to get there.”
  • Digs — “Eighty-five logo files, a fabric specification sheet, and a tech pack a factory can cut from.”
  • Puff Junction — “Thirteen technical drawings, a factory quote, and a six-product line that got made.”

Compare

“A comprehensive branding project for a premium eyewear company, encompassing identity, packaging and art direction.” — true, forgettable, and indistinguishable from every other portfolio on the internet.

5.2 — Naming and register

  • Call the page “Work”. Pentagram, Porto Rocha and Collins all do. “Portfolio” sounds like a job application; “Projects” is Behance's word and carries the platform with it.
  • The cell affordance is “Open the case”, not “View project” or “Learn more”. It names what happens.
  • The shell speaks in one neutral, factual register. Each brand's voice appears inside its own cell as a specimen — quoted, attributed, in its own face. You show “Not just sunglasses. A statement.” You never write the page in it.
  • No view counts, no like counts, no “passionate”, no “crafted”, no “I'm a designer who loves…”. Nothing that would survive being pasted onto someone else's site.
06

Analytical UX — the portfolio is a comparison interface

This is the discipline that looks least relevant here and is actually the differentiator, so it has to earn the space rather than be bolted on.

The reframe: the viewer is running a comparison. Now that the reader is an investor reading range as evidence, the question is not “can this person do my kind of thing?” It is sharper and it is about risk: can this person take something from nothing to a made thing, and is it repeatable or was one of them a fluke?

Three is the smallest number that can answer that. One is luck, two is a coincidence, three across three unrelated categories is a method. The page’s analytical job is to make that pattern legible in seconds rather than leaving it to be inferred across three scrolling case pages.

6.1 — The pattern strip

One dense block, and for this audience it is promoted: it sits directly under the three cells rather than at the foot of the page, under a heading that states the pattern outright — Three categories. Three times. Each one manufactured. The pictures carry the shock; this converts the shock into a claim an investor can check.

The rows are chosen for what underwrites execution risk, not for what a design buyer shops for. “Reached production” is the row doing the work: it is the claim most design portfolios cannot make, and putting it in a table rather than a headline is what keeps it from sounding like a boast.

The row that is doing the persuading

Design register. Monochrome-severe, graphic-confident, pop-art-maximalist — with the pictures directly above proving it. For an investor that row is the answer to “is this a person with one look, or a person with a method?” The aesthetic range is the evidence that what transfers is process rather than taste.

6.2 — A lens, not a filter

A filter across three items is absurd — there is nothing to narrow. A lens is not. Let the viewer re-cut the same three projects by the facet they came for — identity, product, packaging, photography — and each cell re-renders to show that facet of that brand. Same three projects, different evidence. A client who only buys packaging sees three packaging projects.

That is a drill-down rather than a filter, and it is the one place a dense-data background genuinely buys something a visual designer would not have reached for.

Cut this first

It triples the asset requirement — four curated facets per brand instead of one hero. If the imagery is not deep enough in all four, ship the strip in §6.1 alone. It carries most of the value at a fraction of the cost.

6.3 — Three levels, and the third is a locked vault

  • Level 1 — the wall. Three cells. The only decision is which to open.
  • Level 2 — the case. The argument for one brand, in that brand’s own system, illustrated only with photographs of the real product. This is the public ceiling.
  • Level 3 — the artefact index. Every technical drawing, tech pack, fabric spec, STEP file and logo suite — listed by name, count and date, and locked.

The gate is the feature, not the restriction

Your call was public to the edge of real product, gated past it. That is the right instinct, and for an investor it is better than opening everything.

A visible, itemised index of twenty technical drawings you cannot open proves two things at once: that the depth is real, and that you are disciplined about IP. Someone reading execution risk is also reading whether you would be careless with theirs. An unlocked vault answers the first question and fails the second.

  • Public, always: photographs of manufactured product. Today that is the six Darkest Shades frames in §4.5, and only those — Puff Junction has renders rather than production shots, and Digs has neither (§9.1). Nothing else goes above the gate.
  • The one exception, drawn honestly. Puff Junction runs on renders until production photography exists, and the plate says render in the same plain type as everything else. This is not a hedge: an investor who works out unaided that a “photograph” was a render has just learned something about how you handle evidence, and that lesson will outlast the image. Labelling it costs one word and buys the whole page’s credibility.
  • Listed but locked: filename, file type, page or part count, date. Render it as a real index rather than a teaser — Weed_Grinder_Lid_Threaded_V01.pdf · technical drawing · 1pp persuades where the word “confidential” does not.
  • The unlock is a person, not a form. One line — “Ask and I will walk you through these.” For a room this size, a request that reaches you directly beats any gated-download mechanism, and it turns the vault into a reason to start a conversation.
  • Never fake the lock. If a file is on the page in any form, it is public. Blur, low-resolution previews and click-to-reveal are all trivially defeated, and they read as theatre to exactly the audience you are trying to persuade.
07

Constraints the build must respect

  • The asymmetric grid collapses, it does not shrink. Below 1024px the 7/5 pair becomes two full-width bands; Puff Junction stays full-bleed at every width. Do not scale a 7-column cell down to phone width — the art direction that justified the span is gone.
  • §4.1b is a hard rule, not guidance. No Puff Junction colour ever becomes text on the wall — it fills a shape and carries a black label. Digs blue is the one accent that may be type (7.21:1). Every cell carries the 1px ink mount; a pale fill without one has no boundary and the page comes apart.
  • Focus is visible and the takeover is keyboard-reachable. The accent adoption fires on :focus-visible, not hover alone, or the entire signature interaction is mouse-only.
  • Budget the imagery. The source renders are 20MB PNGs at 4000×4000. Serve responsive JPEG/AVIF at three widths, lazy-load everything below the first screen, and hold the initial payload under ~1.5MB. Reserve every image's box with explicit dimensions so the grid never reflows as it loads.
  • Every cell needs all six states drawn: rest, hover, focus, live (accent adopted), loading, and image-failed. The last one matters on a page this image-dependent — a cell whose photograph never arrives must still read as that brand.

Two conditions come with it, and both matter more than the saving. The fonts stop the day the subscription does — the site does not break, it quietly falls to the next face in the stack, which on a page arguing for typographic judgement is worse than breaking. And if this ever ships under someone else’s domain, the web project has to live in their Adobe account, not yours, or their site is running on your seat.

What to actually do, in order

  • Get the traffic number before you email anyone. Every web price here except Adobe’s is a function of a figure you have to state — MCKL wants monthly visitors, Rellence wants monthly pageviews, MyFonts wants a domain. A private investor page is low-traffic by design, so you land in the bottom band of every tier. This is the cheapest this will ever be; buy it while that is true.
  • Check the Adobe seat first. Before spending $350 on Owners, confirm whether a paid Creative Cloud subscription is already live. If it is, this purchase does not happen.
  • Two of them are just checkouts. Stolzl’s family and The Old Falcons are small perpetual desktop purchases plus an annual web fee, both completable in a browser in ten minutes. Do them the day the type is chosen, not the week of the build.
  • Arnet needs an email, not a cart. Its base is a Brand Licence priced on company size, which is a conversation rather than a shopping flow. State the headcount honestly — at yours you sit in the lowest band, and the honest answer is also the cheap one.
  • Budget the renewals, not the stickers. Two of the four are annual and lapse silently. The sticker price is the smaller number; the standing cost is a diary entry.
  • One escape hatch, and it has a boundary. The page needs four commercial faces only because three brands each arrived with their own. If a licence stalls, substitute inside that brand’s cell only, and say so in the plate. The wall’s argument survives a substituted face; it does not survive a face that never loads.
08

Build order

Sequenced so the riskiest assumption gets tested first and the expensive work happens last.

  1. Write the nine lines firstThree plates × three text parts, before any layout. If the evidence lines are not sharp, no grid saves the page — and they take an hour, not a week.
  2. Build one cell, three timesThe same cell component rendered in all three brand systems, on the light wall with its ink mount, at desktop width. This is the whole thesis. If three brands in one frame do not hold here, stop and re-plan rather than build the rest.
  3. Settle the licensingPer §7.1: check the Adobe seat first, then two checkouts and one email. Do this before the type is designed into the layout, not after.
  4. Then the grid, the takeover, the stripIn that order. The comparison strip is last of the three because it is the only one that can ship in a later pass without the page looking unfinished.
